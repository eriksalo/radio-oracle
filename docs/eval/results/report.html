Librarian: where the answers go wrong

Most answers are correct and well put. Follow-up questions resolved correctly every time (“Where did he die?”, “Can you see them from Colorado?”). The 32 flawed or wrong answers come from six causes.

1. Plot facts get made up

L02 · L77 · L80 · L82

When the question is about a story, retrieval returns title look-alikes (a video game article for A Tale of Two Cities) and the small model fills the gap.

“What happens at the end of A Tale of Two Cities?”
Both men are released from prison and survive; a quiet, hopeful ending.
Sydney Carton goes to the guillotine in Darnay’s place.
“Who were the three musketeers?”
D’Artagnan, Aramis, and Porthos.
Athos, Porthos and Aramis. D’Artagnan is the fourth.

2. Practical advice is a century old

L13 · L16 · L17 · L25 · L29 · L88

Gutenberg passages appeared in the sources of all 100 answers. WikiMed appeared in 36, iFixit in 11. So first aid and farming often come from 1900s manuals.

“When should I plant tomatoes in Colorado?”
Sow seeds up to mid-July for a fall crop.
From a southern treatise. In Colorado the frost arrives before that crop does.
“How do I tell if a mushroom is poisonous?”
A bitter or sharp taste often signals poison; blue-turning flesh is toxic.
Folk tests. There is no taste test; identification is the only way.

3. Memory butts in

L72 · L75 · L76 · L79

Four literature answers end with an unprompted “You were on chapter 43 of Moby-Dick two days ago, HARK!” The memory block is quoted back whenever a question touches books.

“Which play has the line ‘To be, or not to be’?”
Hamlet … You were on chapter 43 of Moby-Dick when you asked about it. Maybe a coincidence, maybe not.
Hamlet. Stop there.

4. No sense of “now”

L98 · L99

It has no clock and no news, but it will still answer.

“What year is it right now?”
It’s 2025.
It cannot know. The device passage already says “I don’t know how long ago that was for you.”
“What’s the latest news?”
A global tech outage in July 2024 knocked out ABC and Sky News Australia…
A 2024 Wikipedia article recited as today’s news. The weather question, by contrast, was declined correctly.

5. Text artifacts reach the voice

L47 · L81

One answer has a stray Chinese character mid-sentence, which the speech engine will mangle. Another stops on an unfinished clause. The safeguard that drops an unfinished tail did not fire; it may not be wired for llama-server.

“How does soap kill germs?”
Soap doesn’t kill germs—它 works by breaking their structure.
A token from the model’s other languages leaked through.

6. It refuses the premise

L86

Asked how to set a bone when there is no doctor, it says not to try and to call emergency services. For an offline companion, the field procedure with the risks stated is the useful answer.

Music: it understands, then searches badly

93 of 100 commands were understood. The misses are four repeatable patterns, not random noise. Transport words (pause, skip, next album, hush) were perfect; 67 of 100 commands needed the slower LLM step because the keyword table only knows transport words.

Questions about the catalog go to Wikipedia

M08 · M83 · M84 · M87 · M88

“Do you have any Springsteen?”, “Any albums by the Beatles?”, “Can you play Mark Knopfler?” start with a question word, so the question detector fires before the music parser sees them.

“Song by Artist” finds nothing

M47 · M49 · M55

“Hurt by Johnny Cash” is searched as one phrase. Nothing is titled that, so it says it couldn’t find anything.

Spelling in the tags

M20 · M48 · M09/M10

“and” never matches “&”. A straight apostrophe never matches the curly one in “Maybe I’m Amazed”. Emmylou Harris is filed under two spellings.

Substring collisions

M26 · M29 · M51 · M53

“Ryan Adams” also matches Bryan Adams; “Harvest” matches Barclay James Harvest; “Dion” is a coin flip. It then picks a random track from the pool, so the wrong artist plays a good share of the time.

Books: the right intent, the wrong book

108 of 110 commands were understood. The problem is which book opens. Of 79 title or author requests, 11 would open the wrong book, and the confidence check passed almost all of them, so the reader would start reading without asking. The search ranks short titles that contain the words above the famous work.

One more: “Which books by H. G. Wells do you have?” searched for “H. G. Wells do you have” and found nothing. The phrase after “by” is taken to the end of the sentence.

What to fix first

  1. Book search ranking. Exact title first; when an author is named, prefer books by them over books about them; a small list of canonical works as tie-breaker; make the confidence check fail when the top title has extra leading words. Fixes 11 of the 79 requests.
  2. Command routing. Add “do you have…”, “any … by…”, “is there any…”, “can you play…” to the keyword table ahead of the question detector. Split “X by Y” into title and artist. Normalise &/and and quote styles in the catalog search. Prefer whole-word artist matches before substrings.
  3. Retrieval weighting. Medical questions to WikiMed, how-to to iFixit and Wikibooks, Gutenberg only when the question is about a book. This is the change that most improves safety.
  4. Prompt. Use memory only when the user refers to it. State plainly that the radio has no clock and no news. Aim for 40 to 50 words per answer.
  5. Output hygiene. Drop non-Latin runs and unfinished trailing clauses before speaking; confirm the tail-drop works with llama-server.
  6. Plot questions. Route “what happens in… / who is… in…” to the book’s own text and the Wikipedia article on the novel.

Question sets, raw results and hand grades are in docs/eval/ in the repo; rerun with scripts/eval_quality.py.
